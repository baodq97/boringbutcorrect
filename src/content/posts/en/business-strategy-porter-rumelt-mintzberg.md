An online retailer sets a goal for this year: grow revenue from VND 100 billion to VND 120 billion. On Monday, the CEO signs a plan: promise a delivery date only when stock is certain, and put customers near the warehouse first. On Wednesday, the sales team launches an ad for fast delivery everywhere. This example is hypothetical, but the question is real: which of these is the company's strategy, the goal, the plan, or what sales is actually doing?

**Short answer:** the VND 120 billion figure is not a strategy. By both Porter and Rumelt, a list of financial goals is not one.[^1] For Porter, the core is choosing what not to do.[^2] For Rumelt, strategy is coherent action backed by an argument, built on a diagnosis.[^3] Mintzberg and Waters add one more point: intended strategy is the leaders' intention, while realized strategy (the video from the same episode calls it the real strategy) is the pattern in the actual stream of actions.[^15] This post derives a short test: who and what does this plan give up, and does daily work follow it?

## Where this concept comes from

This post picks five milestones from the Progression section of the source document:

- **1979, Porter, Five Forces in HBR.** Five forces (existing rivals, new entrants, suppliers, buyers, substitutes) analyze industry structure.[^5][^17] Strategy is understood as positioning within that structure.[^6]
- **1985, Mintzberg and Waters.** Realized strategy has a deliberate part and an emergent part. The source document files this paper under the critics of the school that treats strategy as a plan.[^6]
- **1996, Porter, "What Is Strategy?".** Separates operational effectiveness from strategy, and adds trade-offs and fit.[^7]
- **2010, Casadesus-Masanell and Ricart.** Separate strategy, business model and tactics.[^8]
- **2011 and 2022, Rumelt.** The kernel of strategy,[^3] then *The Crux*: focus on the challenge instead of the goal.[^9]

As of the date the source document was last checked (2026-11-04), the definition of strategy still rests on Porter 1996 and Rumelt: choice, trade-offs, fit, diagnosis.[^6]

## Porter: choose differently, choose what to give up, and make activities support each other

Porter opens his 1996 article with *"Operational effectiveness is not strategy"*.[^10] Operational effectiveness means doing the same things as rivals, but doing them better. Strategic positioning means doing different things from rivals, or doing similar things in different ways.[^11]

Porter separates the two because best practice is easy to copy, so every company moves toward the productivity frontier and the advantages cancel each other out.[^10]

This definition does *not* say operational effectiveness is useless.[^11] Porter wrote *"Both are essential, but"* and then explained that the two follow different logics.[^10] Applied to the hypothetical example, this post infers that fixing how shipped goods are recorded may be the thing to do first; it just does not yet make the company different from its rivals.[^12]

Porter's strategy has three parts, each answering one question:[^10]

- **Position: what do we do differently?** *"Strategy is the creation of a unique and valuable position, involving a different set of activities."*[^13] There are three sources of positioning: variety (choose a subset of products or services), needs (serve most or all of the needs of a group of customers), access (customers reached in a different way, for example by geography or scale).[^10]
- **Trade-offs: what do we not do?** *"The essence of strategy is choosing what not to do."*[^2] A position lasts only when trade-offs stop rivals from both keeping their old position and copying yours.[^10]
- **Fit (the video calls it activities that support each other): do the activities support each other?** *"Strategy is creating fit among a company's activities."*[^14] There are three levels: consistency between each activity and the strategy; activities that reinforce each other; optimization of effort across activities. The higher the level, the harder to imitate.[^10]

A good list of tasks, each one right, is still not a strategy if the tasks do not support each other. This is where Porter and Rumelt meet: strategy is a system of activities that fit together.[^1] Applied to the example, this post infers: when the warehouse records shipped goods on time, the website can dare to promise a delivery date; an ad for fast delivery everywhere pulls that whole chain out of line.[^12]

## Rumelt: strategy starts with a diagnosis

Rumelt says the same thing from another angle.[^1] Strategy, in his view, is not ambition, vision or planning, but *"coherent action backed by an argument"*.[^3] The kernel has three parts: diagnosis, guiding policy, and coherent actions.[^3]

The diagnosis explains the nature of the challenge, and simplifies reality by pointing out which factor is critical. The guiding policy is the overall approach to overcoming the obstacles the diagnosis identified. The actions (the figure below calls them the first moves) are the coordinated steps that carry out the guiding policy.[^1]

*The Crux* (2022), according to the publisher's description: focuses on the challenge instead of the goal, and looks for the crux, the part of the challenge that is both surmountable and most promising for big progress.[^9]

This post infers: the two views differ in their starting point. Porter starts from the question of where you stand relative to rivals; Rumelt starts from the question of where you are stuck. A plan can pass by one and fail by the other: it has the right diagnosis but gives up nothing, or it gives something up but the giving up is aimed at no challenge.[^12]

## Mintzberg and Waters: strategy is also in what was done

Mintzberg and Waters (1985) add the dimension of time.[^15] Intended strategy is the leaders' intention. Realized strategy is the pattern in the actual stream of actions, not a document.[^15]

Realized strategy has two parts. The deliberate part is doing exactly what was intended. The emergent part is a pattern that forms without, or against, intention.[^4] These are the two ends of one continuum, not two separate kinds;[^4] actual strategy sits in between.[^15]

This definition does *not* say the emergent part is bad. The source document sums it up: deliberate gives direction, emergent gives learning.[^16] This post infers: when daily work drifts from the plan, the right question is not yet "who did it wrong" but "has anyone looked at this gap".[^12]

Combining this with Casadesus-Masanell and Ricart, the source document draws one consequence: the business model that is running reflects the realized strategy, including the emergent part that no strategy slide records.[^15]

Where they agree: Porter and Rumelt both treat strategy as a choice that excludes options and as a system of activities that fit together.[^1] Where they differ: Porter treats strategy as a deliberate choice of position, while Mintzberg argues that most actual strategy emerges from action.[^16]

## How this concept differs from what it is often confused with

- **A goal.** "Grow 20%" is not a strategy by Porter or by Rumelt.[^1] In our reading, a goal says what you want to reach; it does not say what you will choose and what you will give up.[^12]
- **A plan.** Rumelt separates strategy from planning.[^3] For Mintzberg and Waters, realized strategy is a pattern in action, not a document.[^15]
- **Industry analysis.** Five Forces answers whether an industry is attractive; positioning answers where you stand within it.[^17] Industry structure and a company's relative position are two drivers of profitability.[^5] Technological innovation can change entry and substitution.[^5]
- **Business model and tactics.** A business model is *"a reflection of the firm's realized strategy"*; strategy is a contingent plan about which model to use; tactics are the remaining choices inside the chosen model.[^8] So a Business Model Canvas describes a business model, not a strategy.[^18]
- **Balanced Scorecard and OKR.** Both take strategy as an input and do not diagnose the challenge. A set of OKRs with no diagnosis above it is just a list of goals.[^19]

## Where technology stands in this definition

Henderson and Venkatraman (1993) put forward the Strategic Alignment Model: four domains on two axes, strategic fit (external and internal) and functional integration (business and technology).[^20] The key point is that technology strategy has its own external domain, and the authors reject the view of technology as merely a "cost of doing business".[^21] The model's four perspectives include technology driving business strategy (competitive potential), not only the familiar top-down view of strategy driving technology (strategy execution).[^21]

Two later critiques are worth reading together, because they pull in opposite directions:

- **The alignment trap.** Bain surveyed more than 500 executives. 11% of the companies in the survey fell into the "highly aligned, not highly effective" group: this group spent 13% more on technology but grew 14% slower. Only 8% had effective technology but were not aligned.[^22] Bain concludes that you should make technology effective first and align it afterward.[^23]
- **Fusion.** Bharadwaj and colleagues (2013) propose merging technology strategy and business strategy into digital business strategy, with four themes: scope, scale, speed and sources of value.[^24] Venkatraman co-authored both the 1993 and the 2013 papers, so this is an author updating his own model.[^23]

## What to read carefully

- **Positioning or emergence.** Porter (1996) mentions the criticism that positioning is too static and answers that trade-offs and fit create durability. The source document treats the two views as complementary.[^16] This post reads that as the document's judgment, not something both sides have agreed on.[^12]
- **Alignment or fusion.** The source document holds that fusion suits companies whose products are already digital; for companies with many old core systems, the alignment trap shows that efficiency needs fixing first.[^16] In our reading, this is the document's judgment, not a measured result.[^12]
- **Independence of sources.** Bain is a consulting firm that sells technology services, and its data is self-assessment combined with hard figures.[^23] whatmatters.com is Doerr's own organization.[^25] Read these two sources as words from parties with an interest.
- **Depth of reading.** Porter 1996 was read in full.[^11] Rumelt 2011 only through search excerpts,[^3] Mintzberg and Waters only through the first page,[^4] *The Crux* only through the publisher's description.[^9]
- **The OKR date is still inconsistent.** whatmatters.com and Wikipedia say Doerr brought OKR to Google in 1999;[^25][^26] Google re:Work says "early 2000".[^26]
- **A specific industry.** Strategy and Five Forces for the insurance industry have no industry source yet.[^27]

## Applying it: checking a plan in five steps

The source document has no procedure like the one below. This post assembles it from the definitions above, so each step says whose idea it borrows. The example and all numbers are assumptions.[^12]

1. **Cross out every target number and see what is left** (idea from Porter and Rumelt). In the example, cross out VND 120 billion and what remains is the sentence that promises a delivery date only when stock is certain, customers near the warehouse first. If no sentence remains, the document is a list of goals.
2. **Write the diagnosis as one sentence** (idea from Rumelt). The sentence must point to the critical factor. "Delivery promises are not credible because inventory figures are wrong" is a diagnosis; "we need growth" is not.
3. **Write the "give up" side with its cost in numbers** (idea from Porter). In the example, distant regions bring in VND 24 billion of the VND 120 billion target. Dropping distant regions entirely would leave VND 96 billion. The company chooses differently: it turns away no customer, it only drops the fast-delivery promise for distant regions and promises delivery within five days, with certainty. Suppose one in four distant customers leaves, that is, a quarter of the VND 24 billion from distant regions: a loss of VND 6 billion, leaving a total of VND 114 billion. Still up from today's VND 100 billion, but short of the VND 120 billion target.
4. **Separate what differs from rivals from what rivals can also do, then draw how the activities support each other** (idea from Porter). Fixing how shipped goods are recorded is operational effectiveness: do it first, but rivals can fix it too. The fast-delivery zone (the area around the warehouse, within the area where the company promises fast, certain delivery) is the actual choice. Whatever pulls the chain out of line must change; here that is the ad for fast delivery everywhere: run it only inside the fast-delivery zone.
5. **After one quarter, put the plan next to the real work** (idea from Mintzberg and Waters). In the example, three months later the fast-delivery ad still runs in distant regions; out of every 100 orders from distant regions, 12 are still cancelled. Sales is judged only on revenue, so the real work follows the metric, not the plan. This gap needs a decision with a named owner: fix the plan, or fix the work and add a metric.

This post infers three signs that should make you stop when reading a plan: it has only goals and does not say what is given up; the work it calls strategy is something rivals can also do; daily work runs against the plan and no one has looked at it again.[^12]

For people working in technology, the source document has one consequence, which it labels as the learner's own inference: a technology request should say which strategic choice it serves, not just "supports the goal of growing revenue". If it cannot say, the request either belongs to operational effectiveness (it may still be worth doing, but judged by other criteria), or is just an ambition without a strategy.[^28]
